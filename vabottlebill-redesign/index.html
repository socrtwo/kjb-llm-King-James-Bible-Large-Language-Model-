Virginia’s Litter Crisis

Beverage containers make up 70% of roadside litter in Virginia. Because Virginia is located along the Chesapeake Bay, much of this waste ends up in the ocean.

A Clark County litter study over 7 years found beverage containers made up 69% of the litter they picked up. A 3-year tally of litter picked up in Middleburg, VA found beverage containers also accounted for 70% of roadside litter.

Right now, Virginia recycles only 8% of its PET beverage containers, compared to Maine’s 75% or Oregon’s 71% — both states with bottle bills.

In the 2020 Annual Solid Waste Report published by the Virginia DEQ, it was unveiled that only slightly over 7% of waste processed in permitted facilities was able to successfully be recycled — equivalent to over 1,415,000 tons of solid waste out of the 16,745,894 tons Virginia produced.

What Is a Bottle Bill?

A “Bottle Bill” is slang for “a beverage container deposit and redemption system,” or “deposit system.” Calling it a “Bottle Bill” has a nicer ring to it, but our friends at the Can Manufacturer’s Institute feel ignored.

When you buy a beverage at a store, you pay the store a deposit on each bottle or can. When you return your empty can/bottle to the store or special redemption center, you get the deposit back. There are over 50 Bottle Bills in the world, including 10 states in the USA, with 50 years of experience in what works and what doesn’t.

How It Works

1

You buy a beverage and pay a small deposit (e.g., 10¢) on each bottle or can.

2

You return your empty container to a store, reverse vending machine, or redemption center.

3

You get your deposit back. The container is recycled. Everybody wins.

Reverse Vending Machines: All stores will have “reverse vending machines” to take back containers. Most states also have “Deposit Redemption Centers” where larger quantities of containers can be returned. It is a quick drop off — you do not stand in line and wait for your money. The employees process your redemption and credit your account. This is fast, easy, and convenient.

Why a Bottle Bill?

Deposits place the cost of managing post-consumer beverage containers where it really belongs — on those who manufacture, sell, and buy them. The deposit system shifts those costs to producers and consumers of the containers. This is known as extended producer responsibility (EPR).

Bottlers used to keep their bottles out of the trash and off the sides of streets when they had refillable bottles. They purposely pushed a “nonreturnable” bottle and left the cost of disposal and litter cleanup to people and governments.

🌏

Reduces Litter

The deposit refund rewards every person that picks up litter. Hundreds if not thousands of people start to pick up litter as a result. Overall litter is reduced, not just beverage containers. States have reported a reduction in total litter anywhere from 30–50% and a reduction of beverage container litter ranging from 70–83%.

♻

Triples Recycling

A bottle bill combined with curbside recycling triples the overall amount of beverage containers recycled. While the U.S. national recycling rate stagnated at around 34%, high-performing deposit return systems routinely collect 90% or more of containers for recycling.

🏭

Reduces Landfill Waste

Reduces the trash in landfills and reduces air pollution from incinerators, which lowers Greenhouse Gas Emissions. It is always more efficient to “mine” materials out of the waste stream than to find virgin material in the ground and create new containers.

💰

Supports Charities

Some states let charities sign up, and people’s refunds are donated directly to the charity of their choice. Charities can also sponsor drives. Millions of dollars have been raised this way.

💼

Creates Jobs

Bottle bills create jobs in collection, sorting, processing, and recycling operations across the state, stimulating local economies.

🌊

Protects Waterways

Because Virginia is located along the Chesapeake Bay, much of beverage container waste ends up in the ocean. A bottle bill helps keep containers out of our rivers, streams, and the Bay.

Why Does Virginia Need a Bottle Bill?

  • Virginia currently recycles an estimated 23% of its single-use beverage containers.
  • Beverage containers make up 70% of roadside litter in Virginia.
  • In states with bottle bills, beverage container litter has been reduced by an average of 80%.
  • Northern Virginia’s curbside recycling rates are extremely low at 21%, reflecting the lack of incentive to recycle.
  • The Northern Virginia Region Solid Waste organization report of 2018 showed glass as 0% of all recycled material — it all went to the landfill or incinerators.
  • In the 10 states with bottle bills, total beverage container recycling averaged 71%.
  • Vermont and Alberta, Canada recycle over 90%.
  • Virginia’s PET bottle recycling rate is just 8%, compared to Maine’s 75% or Oregon’s 71%.
  • In states with refund programs, drink containers make up about 6.29% of litter, while in states without these programs, the number is about 14.89%.
  • In Virginia, 16.78% of litter in 2022 came from beverage bottles and cans.

Proposed Legislation

Ten states currently have Bottle Bills, and none of them are the same. The most successful bills share some things in common:

10¢ Deposit on All Beverages

A 10 cent or higher deposit on all beverage containers including beer, wine, liquor (including small “nips”), soda, water (still and carbonated), fruit and vegetable juices, hard ciders, teas, sports drinks, coffee drinks, wellness and functional drinks, foil pouches, cartons (Tetra Paks), and milk.

Easy Returns

Easy returns at the point of sale and larger redemption centers for bulk returns, using Reverse Vending Machines (e.g., TOMRA) at stores and redemption centers. Oregon and Maine have nice return systems Virginia can duplicate.

Distributor-Run System

System run by the distributors, not a new government agency.

90% Return Rate Target

Return rate of 90%, with penalties for lower return rates.

Self-Funding

Funded by the uncollected deposits (estimated at $114 Million at 80% return) and not by a handling fee.

Oregon’s Model: BottleDrop

Oregon has a nice system Virginia can duplicate: BottleDrop, Oregon’s Bottle & Can Return Program, instead of reinventing the wheel. Oregon’s Bottle Bill was introduced in 1971 as the very first bottle bill in the U.S. The refund value increased to 10 cents in 2017. According to the Oregon Beverage Recycling Cooperative (OBRC), Oregon achieved a 90.5% redemption rate in 2023. OBRC, a private cooperative owned by retailers and beverage distributors, administers the collection and transportation of returned containers and keeps all the unclaimed deposits.

Myths, Facts & FAQs

“Bottle Bills financially burden consumers.”

Fact: These programs cost consumers nothing. The deposit is fully refundable when consumers return the container.

“Deposits duplicate curbside recycling.”

Fact: A bottle bill combined with curbside recycling actually triples the overall amount of beverage containers recycled. They complement, not duplicate, each other.

“Industry can handle this without government intervention.”

Fact: Manufacturers always say they can handle this without government intervention but have always failed to achieve meaningful results. The lobby groups in New York state outspent the pro-bottle bill 100 to 1, fighting it over ten years, but they eventually lost, and New York now has one of the best recycling rates in the country.

“Bottle Bills will damage local businesses.”

Fact: Although opponents contend that bottle bills reduce sales and eliminate jobs, the evidence from all 10 states with bottle bills shows that businesses adapt and thrive. In fact, bottle bills create new jobs in collection, sorting, and processing.

“Bottle Bills are outdated.”

Fact: Bottle Bills have been modernized over the decades. Oregon updated its program in 2017, raising the deposit to 10 cents and modernizing the return infrastructure. High-performing deposit return systems routinely collect 90% or more of containers for recycling.

How do unclaimed deposits work?

Bottle Bills generate millions of dollars in unclaimed deposits each year. These legally are property of the State, who decides how this money is spent. Most states fund the picking up of the bottles from collection points. In Virginia, uncollected deposits are estimated at $114 million at an 80% return rate.

Can I return bottles from another state?

No. In order to earn a refund, you must have paid a deposit first. The deposit-refund system is based on the idea that a bottle is bought in the state, the deposit is paid, and then the deposit is returned to the purchaser when the container is returned. Containers bought in another state cannot be returned for a refund because no deposit was originally paid in that state. It is illegal to try to collect a refund on a bottle or can purchased in another state.

The Economic Powerhouse of Recycling

In a recent study conducted by the Resource Recycling System, it was revealed that in 2020 Connecticut’s residential recycling contributed to over $1,159 million in the state’s economy. This sum consists of the jobs directly and indirectly implemented as a result of the recycling system.

In 2020, the bottle bill contributed to more than $160 million (14%) in the state’s economy. If a bottle bill can produce $160 million in a small state like Connecticut, imagine what this would entail if all 50 states adopted them.

Massachusetts expected increasing jobs by 1,500 when they revised their bottle law in 2012, and in the 2018 report by Industrial Economics Inc, they counted 1,260 Full Time Equivalent jobs and a ripple effect of 1,700 jobs worth $24 million in wages and benefits.

A bottle bill that includes a return incentive and curbside recycling has been shown to reduce litter and on average ensures 80% of bottles in circulation are recycled or reused.

About Us

The Virginia Bottle Bill Organization was started by three dog walkers that were fed up with litter they found on their daily walks. Their concern for the environment, frustration over Virginia’s lousy recycling rates, and the production of ever-increasing mountains of single-use plastic and trash, made them band together to fight for change.

Many concerned individuals, environmental groups, and manufacturers are fighting to reduce the use of plastic, and they all support the idea of a bottle bill. Still, none had taken the lead to make it happen — so this organization stepped in to fill the void.

The lobby groups in New York state outspent the pro-bottle bill 100 to 1, fighting it over ten years, but they eventually lost, and New York now has one of the best recycling rates in the country.

Rick Galliher

As a dog owner who walked local streets for 20 years picking up litter — mostly single-use beverage containers — Rick found that fellow dog walkers and neighbors were all frustrated about the litter, leading to the conclusion that an organized coalition is needed to prove popular support for a deposit bill.
